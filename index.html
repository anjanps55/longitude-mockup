Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)
Diagnosed Feb 2026 · Updated today
🧬
Genetic Testing May Change Your Treatment
8-35% of FSGS cases have a genetic cause. Genetic FSGS generally does not respond to steroids. In one study, 50% of patients with genetic variants received unnecessary immunosuppression.
Source: Kidney International, 2024
Key Facts
  • FSGS accounts for 40% of adult nephrotic syndrome
  • Over 50 monogenic causes of steroid-resistant FSGS identified
  • Genetic testing can prevent months of unnecessary treatment
  • APOL1 high-risk variants found in ~14% of African Americans
Questions for Your Nephrologist
Priority 1
"Should I get genetic testing before starting immunosuppressive therapy?"
8-35% of FSGS is genetic and steroid-resistant. Testing can prevent ineffective treatment. (Kidney International, 2024)
Priority 2
"What specific genetic panel should be ordered for FSGS?"
Exome sequencing found diagnostic variants in 29% of adults with FSGS. (JASN, 2023)
Priority 3
"If I have genetic FSGS, what are the treatment alternatives?"
Genetic FSGS requires targeted management. (Nature Reviews Nephrology, 2024)
Also Ask
"Should my family members be tested for hereditary implications?"
Genetic counseling recommended for all confirmed genetic FSGS cases. (KDIGO, 2025)

Condition Briefing
Stage 4 Colorectal Cancer
Metastatic CRC · Updated today
⚠️
Molecular Profiling Is Not Optional
Molecular profiling determines whether you qualify for immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or clinical trials that could change your outcome.
Questions for Your Oncologist
Priority 1
"Has my tumor been tested for MSI/MMR status, and am I eligible for immunotherapy?"
KEYNOTE-177 trial showed pembrolizumab achieved 16.5-month PFS in MSI-H mCRC patients. Only ~5% of mCRC is MSI-H, but for those patients, immunotherapy is transformative.
Priority 2
"Has comprehensive molecular profiling been done — KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, HER2, NTRK, TMB?"
NCCN 2025 guidelines mandate comprehensive biomarker testing for all mCRC patients. Approximately 50% of tumors are KRAS mutant, which excludes anti-EGFR therapy and directs treatment toward alternative targeted agents.
Priority 3
"Should I be tested for DPD deficiency before starting 5-FU/capecitabine?"
The EMA mandates DPYD testing before fluoropyrimidine therapy. 3-8% of patients carry DPYD variants causing potentially fatal toxicity. This is entirely preventable with pre-treatment genotyping.
Advocacy Failure
🚨
Up to 30% of mCRC patients may not receive comprehensive biomarker testing.
Without full molecular profiling, patients may miss eligibility for immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or clinical trials that could significantly improve outcomes. Ask your oncologist to confirm all recommended biomarkers have been tested.
Condition Briefing
Gallstones
Cholelithiasis · Updated today
💡
Most Gallstones Don't Need Treatment
Most gallstones (80%) are asymptomatic and don't need treatment — but knowing the warning signs of cholangitis or pancreatitis could save your life.
Questions for Your Doctor
Priority 1
"Are my stones symptomatic enough for surgery, or is watchful waiting appropriate?"
Only 1-2% of asymptomatic gallstone patients develop symptoms per year. NIH and SAGES guidelines support watchful waiting for incidental, asymptomatic stones. Surgery is indicated for biliary colic, not silent stones.
Priority 2
"Could a stone be in my bile duct? Do I need MRCP or ERCP?"
MRCP has near-100% negative predictive value for common bile duct stones, making it ideal for ruling out choledocholithiasis non-invasively. ERCP carries a 5-10% complication risk (pancreatitis, perforation) and should only be used therapeutically, not diagnostically.
Priority 3
"What are the warning signs that I need to go to the ER immediately?"
Charcot's triad (fever + RUQ pain + jaundice) indicates cholangitis requiring emergency intervention. Reynolds' pentad adds confusion and hypotension, indicating septic cholangitis with high mortality if untreated.
Red Flags
🚨
Fever + RUQ pain + jaundice = ER immediately
This combination (Charcot's triad) suggests ascending cholangitis, a life-threatening infection of the biliary system. Do not wait for a scheduled appointment. Call 911 or go to the emergency department.

Kidney Stones
Nephrolithiasis · Updated today
⚠️
Prevention Is Undertreated
Only 7% of high-risk stone patients receive the metabolic workup that could prevent their next stone. Stone composition determines everything.
Questions for Your Urologist
Priority 1
"Was my stone sent for composition analysis, and what type is it?"
AUA/EAU guidelines recommend stone composition analysis for all retrieved stones. Treatment differs dramatically by type: calcium oxalate (dietary changes, thiazides), uric acid (alkalinization, allopurinol), struvite (infection treatment), cystine (tiopronin, hydration).
Priority 2
"Should I have a 24-hour urine metabolic workup?"
Only 7% of recurrent stone formers receive a 24-hour urine collection, despite AUA guidelines recommending it for all recurrent stone formers. This test identifies specific metabolic abnormalities (hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, hypocitraturia) that can be treated to prevent recurrence.
Priority 3
"Could I have primary hyperparathyroidism?"
Primary hyperparathyroidism causes approximately 5% of kidney stones and is completely treatable with parathyroidectomy. PTH levels are rarely checked in stone patients despite guidelines. A simple blood test for calcium and PTH can identify this curable cause.
Advocacy Failure
🚨
93% of recurrent stone formers treated reactively rather than preventively.
Most patients receive surgery for each stone episode without ever getting the metabolic workup that could prevent future stones. Demand a 24-hour urine collection and stone composition analysis. Prevention is possible.

Perimenopause
Hormonal Transition · Updated today
💡
Your Symptoms Are Real
Nearly 40% of perimenopausal women report feeling misdiagnosed. Over 50% receive a psychiatric diagnosis before perimenopause is considered. Your symptoms are real.
Questions for Your Doctor
Priority 1
"Could this be perimenopause, not anxiety/depression/thyroid?"
Over 50% of perimenopausal women receive a psychiatric diagnosis before perimenopause is considered. Only 42% of clinicians initiate discussion about menopause symptoms. Symptoms overlap significantly with anxiety, depression, and thyroid disorders.
Priority 2
"Am I a candidate for HRT, and which type?"
In 2025, the FDA removed misleading black box warnings on HRT based on the flawed 2002 WHI study. Transdermal estradiol is safer than oral (no VTE risk increase). The timing hypothesis shows maximum benefit when started within 10 years of menopause onset.
Priority 3
"When should I get a bone density scan?"
Women lose up to 10% of bone mass in the first 5 post-menopausal years, with the fastest bone loss occurring 2-3 years around the final menstrual period. Early DEXA scanning can identify women at highest risk before fractures occur.
Key Advocacy Update
✅
In 2025, the FDA removed misleading black box warnings on HRT.
The warnings were based on the flawed 2002 WHI study that used older oral formulations in women over 60. Modern transdermal HRT started in the perimenopause window has a favorable risk-benefit profile. Discuss current evidence with your doctor.

What specific genetic test should I ask for?
Longitude AI
Great follow-up. Here's what to discuss with your nephrologist:
1. Clinical exome sequencing for nephropathy genes — This is the most comprehensive single test. A 2023 study found diagnostic variants in 29% of adults with FSGS using whole exome sequencing. It covers over 50 known monogenic causes of FSGS.
2. APOL1 genotyping — Particularly important as APOL1 high-risk variants are found in ~14% of African Americans and are a leading cause of FSGS in this population. This is a targeted, less expensive test.
3. Targeted nephropathy gene panel — If cost is a concern, a focused panel (NPHS1, NPHS2, WT1, TRPC6, INF2, ACTN4) covers the most common genetic causes.
Questions to bring to your visit
1. "Can we order exome sequencing before starting prednisone?"
2. "Should I get APOL1 genotyping?"
3. "If testing takes time, is a watch-and-wait approach safe?"
